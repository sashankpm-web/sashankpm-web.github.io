Writing · Regulated Products

Compliance is a product requirement, not a gate

In regulated products, compliance works better as part of the product than as a review just before launch.

In regulated products, compliance works better when it is treated as part of the product rather than as a review that happens just before launch.

I've seen projects become slower when the product is designed first and regulatory requirements are introduced later. The team then has to change the experience, rewrite acceptance criteria, retest integrations, and sometimes revisit architecture.

My approach has been to bring those conversations into discovery and backlog refinement.

A good example is the Privacy Preferences platform I own for a large U.S. retail bank.

Privacy Preferences and Email Marketing Preferences are separate product areas, but both involve customer consent, regulatory requirements, and multiple enterprise integrations. As new requirements are introduced, the challenge isn't simply building another screen. We need to understand what the requirement means for the customer experience, the data, the APIs, downstream systems, and the teams responsible for operating the product.

For FCRA preferences, for example, we translated the regulatory requirement into specific customer capabilities, system behavior, acceptance criteria, and test scenarios. That allowed Product, Compliance, Engineering, and QA to work from the same definition of what "done" meant.

The same principle applies to GLBA and TCPA-related initiatives. Instead of starting with a generic requirement such as "support compliance," we break it down into measurable product behavior.

For me, three practices make this work:

  • Translate regulations into testable product requirements. A regulation should ultimately result in something a customer, banker, system, or tester can verify.
  • Identify system and stakeholder dependencies early. A regulatory change rarely affects only the screen the customer sees.
  • Make compliance part of prioritization and release planning. Regulatory deadlines, operational readiness, communications, testing, and downstream dependencies all influence when a capability can safely go live.

This doesn't remove the complexity of regulated delivery. It moves the important conversations earlier, when changes are easier to make.

The goal isn't to "get through compliance."

The goal is to build a product where compliance is part of how the product works.
